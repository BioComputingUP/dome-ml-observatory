Reusability Pathway

After investing time creating an AI model and corresponding dataset, it is important to ensure these are reusable in order to maximize the impact of your work. This crucial step is vital to ensure that other researchers in the scientific community can easily find, access, and understand, your outputs for reuse. This example pathway outlines the key steps leveraging OSAI reuse guidelines (R1-R3) to effectively share your AI model and dataset for reuse, thereby enhancing collaboration and accelerating scientific progress.

1 Learn Reuse Best Practices Through Training (R3)

Before sharing your model and data, it's beneficial to understand the established best practices for making research outputs reusable. Several resources provide guidance on data and software management.

Key Resources:
RDMkit (Research Data Management Toolkit)
This toolkit offers comprehensive guidance on managing research data throughout its lifecycle. While not exclusively focused on AI, its principles are fundamental for preparing your AI datasets for sharing.
RSQkit (Research Software Quality Toolkit)
As AI models are a form of research software, this toolkit provides valuable best practices for ensuring your model's code is high-quality, well-documented, and sustainable for long-term reuse.
SciLifeLab Funder requirements and FAIR ML models
This is a practical example of guidance from a national life science organization on how to make machine learning models Open and FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable). It can serve as a template for meeting the requirements of funders and institutions.

2 Create Comprehensive Metadata Based on Standards (R1)

Metadata is the data describing your AI model and dataset. It is essential for reusability. It provides the necessary context for others to discover, comprehend, and trust your work. It allows both humans and machines to interact with them in a structured and consistent way when using metadata standards.

Before you start, it's wise to identify a few potential registries where you plan to deposit your model and dataset (see Step 3). Review their specific metadata requirements to ensure you capture all necessary information from the outset, which will save you time and effort later.

Key Resources:
Metadata for Your Model:
FAIR4ML (FAIR for Machine Learning) Schema
Developed by the Research Data Alliance, this schema is specifically designed to apply the FAIR principles to machine learning models, ensuring they are well-described for reuse.
Model Cards
This is a documentation framework for reporting on a model's intended uses, performance, limitations, and ethical considerations. It provides a standardized summary for potential users.
ONNX (Open Neural Network Exchange)
While a format for model interoperability, ONNX files also embed important metadata about the model's architecture, inputs, and outputs, which is crucial for practical reuse.
Metadata for Your Dataset:
Croissant
A metadata format from MLCommons designed to make ML datasets easily discoverable, understandable, and usable across different tools and platforms.
Dataset Cards
Similar to Model Cards, these provide a structured way to document a dataset's origins, contents, collection methods, and potential biases, helping others assess its suitability for their needs.
PROV-O (PROV Ontology)
The PROV Ontology is a general-purpose standard for representing provenance information. Using it allows you to describe the origin and processing history of your data, building trust and enabling reproducibility.

3 Deposit in an Accessible Registry (R2)

Once your AI model and related dataset are well-documented with rich metadata, the final step is to place them in a public registry to make them accessible to the wider community. Central repositories are crucial for managing the lifecycle of machine learning models. Choose a registry according to aspects such as your target user audience, domain specificity of the registry, and optimal functionality (e.g. metadata supported).

Key Resources:
Registries for Your Model:
Hugging Face
A widely used platform that serves as a central hub for sharing and discovering open-source models, especially in the domain of Natural Language Processing. It has strong community features and integrates well with many popular ML libraries.
Ersilia Model Hub
A specialized registry focusing on pre-trained AI/ML models for drug discovery and infectious disease research, making it an excellent choice for models within this life science domain.
Zenodo
A general-purpose open-access repository, operated by CERN, that can be used to share any research output, including AI models. It is particularly useful for obtaining a persistent Digital Object Identifier (DOI) for your model, which makes it easily citable.
Registries for Your Dataset:
BioImage Model Zoo
A community-driven repository primarily for sharing pre-trained artificial intelligence models for bioimage analysis. It can host reference datasets used to train and test these models, linking models to their corresponding data.
Figshare
An online digital repository where researchers can preserve and share their research outputs, including datasets, figures, and papers, with persistent identifiers.
Kaggle
A platform that hosts a vast collection of datasets, often used for machine learning competitions and data science practice. It fosters a large community for collaboration and provides integrated cloud-based tools for data analysis.

Reproducibility Pathway

To ensure the findings from your AI model are credible and can be built upon by the scientific community, it is essential to move beyond simple reusability and strive for full reproducibility. This pathway guides you through the process of standardizing your evaluations, packaging your code for easy execution, and transparently disclosing all relevant details so that others can independently verify and replicate your results.

1 Standardize Datasets and Evaluation for True Comparison (R6)

Before others can reproduce your model's training, they must first be able to reproduce its performance claims. This requires using standardized, community-accepted datasets and evaluation protocols to ensure that your results are meaningful and can be fairly compared to other methods.

Instead of using private or uniquely processed data for final evaluation, benchmark your model on established "AI-ready" datasets. These datasets have been prepared and agreed upon by the community, reducing variability in evaluation.

Platforms hosting community benchmarking challenges are excellent sources for standardized datasets and evaluation protocols. Participating in or using resources from these challenges ensures your model is assessed against the current state-of-the-art in a controlled and transparent manner.

Benchmarking services like OpenEBench serve as central hubs in the life sciences for discovering benchmarking challenges and accessing their standardized evaluation results and datasets. Using such platforms helps you align your work with community best practices.

Key Resources:
Kaggle
A domain-agnostic platform hosting a vast number of competitions with standardized datasets and evaluation metrics that can be used for benchmarking.
CAGI (Critical Assessment of Genome Interpretation)
A life-science-specific challenge that provides datasets and standardized protocols for models related to interpreting genomic variation.
CAID (Critical Assessment of Intrinsic Disorder prediction)
A life-science-specific challenge that provides datasets and standardized protocols for models that predict intrinsically disordered regions in proteins.

2 Create Reproducible Environments and Workflows (R5)

Once your model has been fairly benchmarked, the next step is to package the entire computational environment and workflow so that others can execute your code with minimal effort and achieve the same results.

Containerize the Environment: The foundation of practical reproducibility is containerization, which bundles your code with all its software dependencies (libraries, tools, and specific versions) into a single, portable image. This eliminates the common problem of "it works on my machine" by ensuring an identical environment on any system.

Key Resources:
Docker
The most widely used containerization platform, ideal for creating and sharing application environments.
Apptainer (formerly Singularity)
A container system popular in academic and High-Performance Computing (HPC) environments, designed with a focus on security for multi-user systems.
Podman
An open-source, daemonless container engine that is a popular alternative to Docker. It enhances security by not requiring a root-privileged daemon and is largely command-compatible with Docker, making it easy to adopt.

Orchestrate with Workflow Management Systems: For complex AI models that involve multiple steps (e.g., preprocessing, training, inference, and evaluation), a workflow management system is crucial. These systems take your containerized components and automate the execution of the entire pipeline in the correct order, ensuring a complete and seamless reproduction of your analysis.

Key Resources:
Nextflow
A powerful and scalable workflow system that excels at managing complex bioinformatics pipelines and integrates seamlessly with container technologies.
Snakemake
A Python-based workflow management system that is highly flexible and widely adopted in the life sciences for creating reproducible data analysis pipelines.
Galaxy
A web-based platform that allows researchers to build, run, and share complex computational analyses, including machine learning workflows, in a highly accessible and reproducible manner.

3 Ensure Transparent Disclosure and Clear Documentation (R4)

With a standardized evaluation and a portable workflow in place, the final step is to create comprehensive documentation that clearly discloses all information necessary for someone to understand and reproduce your work.

Consult AI disclosure frameworks: Many established checklists and recommendations exist to understand and guide researchers on what information is considered essential for disclosure. These frameworks provide a structured guide to ensure you don't miss any critical details.

Key Resources:
DOME Recommendations
Community-driven guidelines for reporting supervised machine learning in biology, covering aspects from data processing to model evaluation.
Model Openness Framework (MOF)
A domain-agnostic checklist for evaluating the openness and completeness of a machine learning model, covering code, data, and documentation.
Bridge2AI Recommendations
NIH guidance including best practices for making biomedical data AI-ready and for transparently documenting AI methodologies.
Disclose Information Alongside Your Research Assets:
  • In the methods section of your publication.
  • As supplementary material linked to your paper.
  • Within the README.md file of your public code repository.
Leverage Dedicated Reporting Services: For an even higher level of transparency and findability, use specialized registries designed to capture structured information about your study.
Key Resource:
DOME Registry
Allows you to create a structured, machine-readable record of your machine learning study. It complements your publication by providing curated, persistent annotations based on the DOME recommendations, directly linking all components of your work and maximizing its visibility and reproducibility.

Environmental Sustainability Pathway

Achieving results with AI should not come at an unnecessary cost to our planet. By embedding environmental sustainability into your research practice, you can minimize the carbon footprint of your work while promoting a more responsible and efficient approach to AI development. This pathway will guide you through implementing Green AI techniques, making informed hardware choices, and transparently reporting your model's environmental impact.

1 Implement Green AI Model Development Techniques (R7)

The most direct way to reduce your environmental impact is to decrease the amount of computation required to train and run your model. Adopting "Green AI" practices focuses on efficiency, ensuring you achieve your research goals with the least amount of energy possible.

Select Efficient Algorithms and Models: Before starting, consider the inherent efficiency of the algorithms you plan to use. Some models are less computationally intensive by design. For tabular data, libraries like XGBoost and LightGBM are not only high-performing but are also optimized for speed and efficiency, making them a greener choice than more complex deep learning models where not necessary.

Key Resource:
XGBoost
A high-performance, efficient library for gradient boosting on tabular data, optimized for speed and resource usage.

Apply Model Optimization Techniques: You can significantly reduce the computational cost of deep learning models by making them smaller and more efficient without a major loss in performance. Techniques like pruning (removing unnecessary model parameters) and quantization (using lower-precision numerical formats) can drastically cut down on resource needs. The EfficientBioAI package is an example of a tool designed specifically to implement such methods for bio-imaging AI tasks.

Key Resource:
EfficientBioAI
A package for bio-imaging AI that implements pruning and quantization techniques to reduce model size and energy consumption.

Adopt Energy-Efficient Development Practices: How you develop your model matters just as much as the model itself. Consult established guides to learn about and apply these practices throughout your project lifecycle. The Green Software Patterns guide, from the Green Software Foundation, provides a collection of actionable strategies, such as efficient data loading, energy-aware hyperparameter tuning, and leveraging transfer learning from pre-trained models to avoid training from scratch.

Key Resource:
Green Software Patterns
A guide from the Green Software Foundation with practical strategies for energy-efficient AI development, including efficient data loading and transfer learning.

2 Choose and Optimize Hardware to Reduce Environmental Impact (R8)

The hardware you use and where it is located are critical factors in your project's overall energy consumption and carbon footprint.

On-Premises and Local Computing: If you are using your institution's hardware, there are still choices you can make to optimize for sustainability. Use modern, energy-efficient hardware like GPUs for deep learning, optimize your code for the available infrastructure, and consider the energy efficiency of the supercomputing centers you use by consulting resources like the Top 500 Green list.

Key Resources:
NVIDIA GPU Cloud (NGC)
A hub for GPU-optimized software that accelerates AI on energy-efficient GPUs.
POP CoE
Provides free services to help academic and industrial users optimize their HPC applications for better performance and efficiency.

Cloud Computing: Cloud platforms offer significant flexibility for reducing your carbon footprint. You can choose to run your computations in data centers powered by a higher percentage of renewable energy and schedule your jobs to run at times when the local electricity grid is less carbon-intensive.

Key Resources:
Carbon free energy for Google Cloud
Guidance from Google to help users select cloud regions with a higher percentage of carbon-free energy.
Carbon Aware SDK
A toolkit that allows applications to be scheduled and run during times of lower grid carbon intensity.

3 Measure and Report AI Model Environmental Impact (R9)

To understand your project's environmental footprint and contribute to a community-wide culture of transparency, it is essential to measure and report your energy consumption and carbon emissions. This should be planned from the very beginning of your project.

Use Active Monitoring Tools (Preferred): Integrate monitoring tools directly into your code to automatically track energy consumption and estimate the carbon footprint of your training and inference runs in real-time. Cloud platforms also offer built-in dashboards to monitor your carbon footprint.

Key Resources:
CodeCarbon
An open-source Python package to track and visualize the energy consumption and CO2 emissions of your code.
Experiment Impact Tracker
A tool to track energy usage, carbon emissions, and compute utilization of your experiments.
Carbon Tracker
A tool designed to predict and track the carbon footprint of training deep learning models.

Estimate Impact When Active Monitoring Is Not Possible: If you cannot use active monitoring tools, you can still create an estimate after your experiments are complete. This requires you to diligently log key details during your runs: the specific hardware used, the total runtime, and the geographic location of the hardware. This information can then be input into an online calculator.

Key Resource:
ML CO2 Impact Calculator
A calculator for estimating the carbon footprint of machine learning experiments based on hardware, runtime, and location.

Report Your Findings: Finally, make your environmental impact metrics public. Disclose them in the methods section of your publication or in your code repository. For a greater community impact, submit your results to dedicated platforms that track and compare the energy efficiency of models, such as the Reinforcement Learning Energy Leaderboard or the Machine Learning Energy Leaderboard.
